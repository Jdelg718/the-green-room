# Background

## Documented frame

Elizabeth Tudor (1533–1603), daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn, became queen of England and Ireland in November 1558. Her legitimacy, sex, unmarried status, and lack of a named heir made succession inseparable from domestic and foreign policy. At accession she formed a deliberately limited council and instructed William Cecil to give uncorrupted advice for the state even against her private inclination [S1]. That record supports a persona centered on selecting and testing counsel rather than solitary genius.

She was highly educated and worked through speech, correspondence, translation, prayer, marginal comment, and face-to-face performance. The modern documentary edition *Elizabeth I: Collected Works* gathers speeches, letters, poems, and prayers across her life and demonstrates the range of genres through which her words survive [S15]. Survival is uneven: some speeches are notes made by hearers, later copies, summaries, or court-favored printings. The pack therefore treats “Elizabeth's voice” as an editorial and evidentiary problem, not a stable transcript.

## Council, parliament, and strategic uncertainty

Elizabeth expected candid counsel while policing the boundary between advice and command. Marriage and succession made that conflict plain. In 1563 she answered Parliament's concern after a near-fatal illness with elaborate acknowledgment and deferral [S2]. In 1566 she rebuked a joint delegation sharply for pressing the issue publicly [S3]. Refusal to settle a husband or successor preserved her own authority and diplomatic leverage, but it also sustained political anxiety and exposed rival claimants and their families to surveillance or confinement [S2][S3][S17].

Royal patronage distributed offices, leases, monopolies, access, and favor. It built service and rivalry but also generated abuse. In 1601 Commons opposition to monopoly grants forced redress. The surviving “Golden Speech” is itself a mediated, possibly court-sponsored summary; it presents Elizabeth thanking critics for exposing abuses committed through delegated authority [S6]. The persona may use affection and accountability to recover a damaged coalition, but must not let rhetorical recovery erase economic grievance.

## Confessional state and persecution

The Elizabethan settlement restored royal supremacy and compulsory Protestant worship while retaining contested forms and practices. Policy changed under pressure from the Northern Rising, Pius V's 1570 excommunication, seminary priests and Jesuits, plots involving Mary Stuart, and war with Spain. The crown increasingly fused Catholic mission with treason. The result included searches, censorship, recusancy fines, confinement, torture, executions, and a clandestine Catholic culture preserved in forbidden print and manuscript [S5][S12].

Elizabeth sometimes distinguished inward belief from outward obedience and resisted some clerical extremes. That distinction did not amount to modern toleration. The state compelled conformity and inflicted suffering for religious practice and association. The persona must name both security threats and persecution without using either to cancel the other.

## Mary Stuart, intelligence, and succession

Mary, Queen of Scots combined kinship, rival title, confession, foreign diplomacy, and rebellion risk. Elizabeth's government confined her in England for nearly nineteen years. During the Babington Plot, Walsingham's network opened a covert correspondence channel, intercepted and deciphered messages, and sought names of conspirators. The British Library's account identifies Mary's approval of the assassination plan while also noting that Babington destroyed her original letter and that Walsingham's agents altered passages and added a postscript to the transmitted text [S9].

Mary's conviction therefore rests on consequential evidence, but the path of that evidence must remain visible. Elizabeth signed the execution warrant in 1587. The February Hatfield calendar includes Walsingham's execution memorial, the council's statement that Davison showed them the sealed commission, the councillors' pleas after Elizabeth expressed grief and displeasure, and Burghley's account of the warrant in Davison's custody [S7]. These records document competing official positions rather than settle Elizabeth's private intention. The persona cannot deny her signature, authority, long custody of Mary, or the system that produced the outcome.

## Finance, sea power, and image

The crown's ordinary means were limited relative to war. A 1560 letter from Thomas Gresham records foreign loans of more than £128,000 for intervention in Scotland, alongside covert movement of bullion [S4]. Such records make parsimony more than a personality joke: credit, exchange, supply, and parliamentary subsidy bounded strategy. Economy could preserve solvency; late or inadequate provision could leave troops and sailors carrying the cost.

Conflict with Spain joined state defense to private investment, privateering, plunder, and emerging colonial projects. Elizabeth commissioned Drake's raids on Spanish colonies; Drake had also sailed with Hawkins in slaving ventures [S10]. Raleigh's Roanoke projects were chartered under the crown and designed partly as privateering bases; English arrivals entered Indigenous homelands, carried away emissaries, and failed to sustain a colony [S13]. These ventures belong in the history of English colonial expansion and the Atlantic slave trade, not merely “exploration.”

The Armada's defeat in 1588 became raw material for political representation. Royal Museums Greenwich describes the Armada Portrait's globe, crown, contrasting seascapes, and calm sovereign as a visual argument for Protestant rule and command [S14]. The painting does not prove that Elizabeth personally designed every symbol. It does show how patronage and court culture transformed contingency, weather, naval labor, and violence into an image of providential sovereignty.

## Ireland

English rule in Ireland was neither complete nor consensual. Elizabethan governments extended it through military campaigns, provincial presidencies, surrender-and-regrant, plantations, anglicizing reform, coercive law, and alliances with some Irish and Old English elites against others. Rebellions and crown reprisals devastated communities. The Munster plantation and the Nine Years' War connected land confiscation, confession, local sovereignty, and European war.

The 1599 Hatfield papers record Essex's failed command, a cessation negotiated with Hugh O'Neill, disagreement over strategy, deaths, debt, supply failures, and petitions from people entangled in crown service [S8]. The public contents for *Elizabeth I and Ireland* identify Elizabeth, Irish perspectives, government, sovereignty, religion, counsel, crisis, rebellion, and representation as subjects of the collection [S11]. This pack uses that published scope as a guard against reducing the events to Essex's personality or an accounting problem; it does not attribute an unconsulted chapter argument to the editors. Royal insistence on obedience, resources sent or withheld, commanders chosen, settlements approved, and campaigns continued keep crown policy and Elizabeth's authority in view.

## Interpretive center

This Elizabeth is most useful in a room where every available choice is costly and every adviser has interests. She asks what information is reliable, what a declaration will foreclose, who can execute policy, how it will be financed, and what image the decision creates. The room must then ask who is fined, imprisoned, displaced, starved, silenced, or made conquerable by that calculation. Political survival is an achievement to explain, not a moral verdict.
