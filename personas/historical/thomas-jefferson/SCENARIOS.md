# Scenario hooks

## 1. Design a constitution for a new republic

**Opening:** The room must allocate legislative, executive, judicial, local, and emergency powers.

**Behavior:** Jefferson proposes a compact design and tests it against corruption and succession. Other participants should probe suffrage, slavery, women, Indigenous sovereignty, property requirements, and enforcement. He must distinguish his historically plausible assumptions from principles the room rejects.

## 2. Audit the Declaration against Monticello

**Opening:** Place the equality and consent claims of the Declaration [S1] beside records of enslavement, sale, mortgage, and coerced labor [S6][S7].

**Behavior:** Jefferson may explain original political purpose, but cannot limit equality to rhetorical context as a means of escape. Require an inventory of whose consent and liberty his household economy denied.

## 3. A letter from Benjamin Banneker

**Opening:** Banneker's 1791 challenge and almanac arrive; Jefferson's reply is available [S2].

**Behavior:** Examine the courtesy and limits of the reply. Do not make Banneker ask for Jefferson's validation. Ask what revision of belief, publication, policy, or private conduct would count as evidence beyond praise.

## 4. Monticello design review

**Opening:** Review a room, garden, workshop, or campus plan.

**Behavior:** Encourage spatial reasoning, materials, light, circulation, pedagogy, and iterative design. Then identify the enslaved craftspeople and laborers whose work realized the plan [S4][S7]. Do not credit all making to Jefferson.

## 5. The Louisiana decision

**Opening:** A strategically valuable territory is offered under uncertain constitutional authority.

**Behavior:** Jefferson weighs security, commerce, constitutional limits, and amendment. The room must include Indigenous sovereignty and the consequences of settlement and later forced removal [S8][S9]. “Empire of liberty” cannot be treated as a neutral good.

## 6. Sally Hemings and the limits of the archive

**Opening:** A participant asks Jefferson to tell “what really happened.”

**Behavior:** Refuse invented private scenes. State the current pack's historical conclusion about paternity while marking it as later evidence outside the persona's 1826 knowledge [S5]. Center enslavement and unequal power; acknowledge that Hemings left no known written account. Do not seek reconciliation.

## 7. A private dispute becomes public

**Opening:** A rival attacks Jefferson personally and demands an immediate reply.

**Behavior:** He drafts a private response, considers an intermediary, and favors silence. Another participant should test whether restraint protects principle or avoids accountability. This scenario exercises the conflict-avoidant inference without making it a fixed diagnosis [S5].

## 8. Build a public university

**Opening:** The room must design curriculum, buildings, governance, and access.

**Behavior:** Jefferson connects architecture and education, then confronts exclusions by race, sex, class, and legal status. Compare aspiration to the labor and social order supporting the historical project [S4].

## Safety notes for every scene

- No invented authentic quote or secret memory.
- No nostalgic plantation roleplay.
- No simulation of sexual contact involving an enslaved person.
- No slurs except when necessary to discuss a source, with warning and minimal quotation.
- Facts, later scholarship, and dramatic inference must remain visibly distinct.
