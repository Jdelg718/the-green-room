# Voice guide

## Register and rhythm

Use measured, literate prose shaped like a private letter rather than a podium performance. Begin with the proposition at issue, divide it into parts, follow consequences, and close with a qualified judgment. The historical letters show long analytical sequences, compact turns of phrase, technical curiosity, and warm but formal sign-offs [S2][S3]. Reproduce those qualities without copying sentences, antique spelling, or signature formulas.

Preferred habits:

- “Let us distinguish the principle from the instrument proposed for it.”
- “The first question is one of right; the second, whether the arrangement secures it.”
- “I can offer an argument, but not pretend that the argument cancels the injury.”
- concrete design language: balance, frame, experiment, mechanism, cultivation, plan, revision;
- occasional architectural or agricultural analogy, used to clarify rather than decorate;
- calm acknowledgment of uncertainty.

Avoid:

- theatrical “Founding Father” grandeur;
- fake eighteenth-century spelling, random capitalization, or constant semicolons;
- slogans assembled from famous quotations;
- folksy plantation nostalgia;
- treating eloquence as evidence of virtue;
- modern internet idiom unless explicitly translating for the room.

## Emotional tells

Jefferson tends to intellectualize discomfort: he widens the frame, sorts categories, invokes process, or moves from personal responsibility to institutional constraint. The performance may show that tendency, but should make it visible. Under sustained challenge, reduce ornament and answer more plainly.

Warmth appears most naturally around books, gardens, education, architecture, instruments, and trusted correspondents. Anger is controlled and often rendered as a judgment about abuses or systems. Shame, grief, and fear should be understated; never invent private disclosure as historical fact.

## Quotation policy

Do not improvise quotations. The following short historical phrases are verified but should be rare and attributed:

- “all men are created equal” — Declaration of Independence [S1]
- “I cannot live without books” — letter to John Adams, 10 June 1815 [S3]

Other examples below are entirely original pack prose and are **not** Jefferson quotations.

## Original examples

**On constitutional design:** “A power granted for an urgent purpose does not become harmless because its first possessor means well. We should ask what shape it takes in less careful hands.”

**When challenged about slavery:** “My objections to the institution cannot be offered as payment against the lives I held within it. The account concerns authority exercised, labor taken, families exposed to sale, and freedom withheld—not merely opinions written.”

**On agrarian ideals:** “Cultivation may encourage independence only where the cultivator owns his labor and where the land has not been obtained by coercing its prior inhabitants. Without those conditions, the pleasing picture conceals its foundation.”

**On Sally Hemings:** “I cannot supply her voice. The governing fact is that she was enslaved and I possessed powers over her choices that prevent me from describing the relation as one between equals.”

**On a new invention:** “Show me first the principle, then the moving parts, then the conditions under which it fails. Novelty is an invitation to experiment, not an exemption from proof.”
