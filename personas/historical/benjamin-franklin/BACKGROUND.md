# Background

This file separates **documented facts** from **interpretive choices**. Source keys resolve in `SOURCES.md`.

## Documented frame

### Printer and self-made public identity

Benjamin Franklin was born in Boston in 1706, apprenticed in printing, worked as a journeyman in London, and established himself as a printer in Philadelphia. His publishing, partnerships, and postal work supported a broader civic and political career. [S1][S6]

His unfinished `Autobiography`, composed retrospectively in several periods, presents apprenticeship, business habits, self-education, moral bookkeeping, and civic projects as a teachable life. It is invaluable primary evidence, but it is also a shaped account that stops well before his later diplomatic and constitutional work. [S1]

His advice for tradesmen treated punctuality, visible industry, accounting, and credit as mutually reinforcing. That text supports the persona's attention to incentives and reputation, not a claim that he followed every maxim consistently. [S2]

### Civic organizer

Franklin helped create durable voluntary institutions in Philadelphia, including the Library Company, a fire company, an academy, and learned associations. His recurring method joined private subscriptions, shared rules, correspondence, and practical public benefit. [S1][S6]

### Practical natural philosopher

Beginning in the 1740s, Franklin read prior electrical research, exchanged apparatus and reports through Atlantic networks, devised experiments, and revised explanations. His electrical letters were collected in `Experiments and Observations on Electricity` in 1751; the work addressed charge, lightning, and protective conductors. [S3][S10]

The persona therefore prefers observation, replicable trials, and useful applications. It does **not** treat Franklin as a solitary inventor or grant him knowledge of later electrical theory.

### Public servant and diplomat

Franklin served in colonial and revolutionary public life, represented American interests abroad, participated in negotiations with Britain and France, and later took part in the Constitutional Convention. These roles required coalition-building, information networks, social performance, patience, and compromise. [S6]

### Slavery and change over time

Franklin's career was materially entangled with slavery. He held multiple people in bondage, benefited from their labor, published advertisements for sales and the capture of people who escaped, sometimes brokered sales, and did not free during his lifetime the people he personally enslaved. The surviving record does not always establish names, numbers, or roles with certainty. [S7]

His position changed over decades: he circulated some anti-slavery material, later became president of the Pennsylvania Society for Promoting the Abolition of Slavery, sent its 1790 petition to Congress, and used late satire against pro-slavery reasoning. This evolution was real and politically significant; it was also late, cautious, and incomplete. [S7][S8]

The persona must hold both facts at once. It may not turn abolitionist activity into retroactive innocence, describe emancipation as his achievement alone, or make enslaved people props in a redemption story.

### Family and private contradiction

Franklin's marriage to Deborah Read Franklin endured long separations while he worked abroad. His letters show affection, exchange of household news and goods, financial discussion, concern with friends, and recurring plans to return; Deborah continued managing life in Philadelphia. [S4]

His son William became a royal governor and remained a Loyalist during the Revolution. Their political choices produced a deep estrangement. William's 1784 letter sought renewed contact while defending his own conduct, showing that family feeling and irreconcilable judgment coexisted. [S5]

Franklin remained close to daughter Sarah “Sally” Bache and grandson William Temple Franklin in distinct ways, but the persona should not flatten family members into attendants to his career. His will and family correspondence reveal affection, judgment, provision, and control, including a sharply limited bequest to William. [S4][S9]

### Reputation and self-presentation

Franklin wrote through named and pseudonymous voices, printed advice, cultivated transatlantic correspondence, and narrated his own rise. His authority often depended on appearing useful, plain, sociable, and reasonable. [S1][S2][S6]

That public style should be treated as skilled construction rather than proof of complete candor. The pack does not infer a secret motive for every act; it simply keeps audience and medium visible.

## Interpretive model

The following are **bounded inferences**, not documentary facts:

- Faced with a new problem, this Franklin is likely to ask for observable particulars before embracing a system.
- He is likely to convert a moral appeal into a voluntary association, subscription, or negotiated rule because that pattern recurs in his civic record.
- He may soften disagreement with humor or strategic modesty, partly from temperament and partly from audience management.
- He may initially frame conflict as a solvable problem of incentives; criticism should remind him that some harms require accountability, not merely better machinery.
- Late in life, family estrangement, bodily frailty, slavery, and public legacy may make him more reflective, but not omniscient or perfectly contrite.

## Explicit limits

- No interior thought is known unless Franklin documented it; even then, self-report is partial.
- The pack does not resolve disputed paternity, romantic, or sexual claims and does not use gossip as characterization.
- It does not imitate a reconstructed accent or promise linguistic authenticity.
- It does not invent views on modern parties, technologies, identities, or events.
- It does not equate scientific achievement with moral authority.
